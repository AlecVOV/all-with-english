# Buddhism and the Esoteric Tradition in Vietnam

> **Lưu ý về niên đại — đọc trước khi làm**
> Có một điểm cần nói rõ: **Mật tông không du nhập vào Việt Nam từ hơn 2000 năm trước.** Đây là con số lưu truyền rất rộng nhưng không đứng vững được về mặt sử liệu, vì hai lý do:
> 1. **Phật giáo** đến Giao Chỉ khoảng **thế kỷ II CN** (~1.800 năm trước), không phải 2.000+ năm.
> 2. **Mật tông (Vajrayāna)** với tư cách một hệ thống có quán đảnh, mạn-đà-la, truyền thừa chỉ **hình thành ở Ấn Độ từ khoảng thế kỷ VII**. Nó chưa tồn tại vào thế kỷ II để mà truyền đi đâu cả.
>
> Bằng chứng chắc chắn sớm nhất ở Việt Nam là dòng Tì-ni-đa-lưu-chi (580) và — quan trọng hơn — **200 cột kinh đá của Đinh Liễn ở Hoa Lư (973 và 979)** khắc chú Phật Đảnh Tôn Thắng.
>
> Bài đọc dưới đây lấy **chính vấn đề niên đại này** làm chủ đề tranh luận học thuật. Đây là điều tốt cho bạn ở hai mặt: nó là dạng lập luận điển hình của Passage 3, và bạn học được lịch sử đúng.

# READING PASSAGE

### Dating a Tradition: Esoteric Buddhism and the Long History of Vietnamese Practice

**A**
Accounts circulating in Vietnam sometimes state that Buddhism, and with it the esoteric strand known locally as Mật tông, reached the country more than two thousand years ago. The claim appears in temple literature, in journalism and occasionally in textbooks. It also conflates two questions that historians are careful to separate: when Buddhism first arrived in the region, and when a recognisably esoteric system of practice arrived. The answers are divided by roughly eight centuries. Far from diminishing the subject, the gap turns out to be the most interesting thing about it.

**B**
On the first question the evidence is comparatively good. By the second century CE, Luy Lâu — in present-day Bắc Ninh province, in the Red River delta — was an established Buddhist centre in Giao Chỉ, the region then under Chinese administration. It was there, in the late second century, that Mâu Tử composed the *Lý hoặc luận*, a defence of Buddhism against contemporary criticism. A generation later Khương Tăng Hội, born in Giao Chỉ to a family of Sogdian merchants, travelled in 247 CE to the kingdom of Wu, where he translated texts and taught meditation on the breath. Significantly, Giao Chỉ lay on the maritime route connecting India with southern China, and a number of historians argue that Buddhism reached it directly by sea, earlier than and independently of the overland transmission into northern China. What none of these early sources contains is any account of initiation, of maṇḍala, or of ritual consecration.

**C**
The manner of reception is as revealing as the date. Local tradition tells of an Indian monk who came to Luy Lâu and whose teaching gave rise to a cult centred on four indigenous weather deities — cloud, rain, thunder and lightning. Each was given a Buddhist name and installed at Chùa Dâu, where they are still venerated collectively as the Tứ Pháp. What this suggests is that Buddhism was received in the delta first as a source of ritual power, capable of doing what local cults already did, and only afterwards as a body of doctrine. The point matters for the chronological question, because it is precisely this early appetite for efficacy that later readers mistake for evidence of tantra.

**D**
The distinction they miss is a technical one but it is decisive. Protective spells, known as dhāraṇī, circulated widely in Mahayana literature from a very early period, long before anything resembling Tantra existed. Their presence in a text or a ritual therefore establishes nothing in particular. Vajrayāna proper — organised around initiation, maṇḍala, consecration and an unbroken transmission from master to disciple — took shape in India only from about the seventh century. A second-century esoteric Vietnam is consequently not merely undocumented but chronologically impossible: the thing said to have been transmitted did not yet exist to be transmitted.

**E**
Firm evidence begins much later. In 580 the monk Vinītaruci arrived at Pháp Vân temple in Luy Lâu and founded the first recorded meditation lineage in the country; the texts associated with his line include dhāraṇī material, which places genuine esoteric elements in Vietnam by the sixth century. The decisive evidence, however, is material rather than textual. In 973, and again in 979, Đinh Liễn, son of the emperor Đinh Bộ Lĩnh, had one hundred stone pillars erected at Hoa Lư on each occasion — two hundred in all — every one of them about 1.4 metres high, carved from green stone and inscribed with the Uṣṇīṣavijaya dhāraṇī. The first was found in 1963, and about twenty were recovered by archaeologists working between then and 1987; they are now held at the Ninh Bình Museum. A comparable but far larger pillar, over four metres tall, was raised by Lê Đại Hành in 995. Dated, inscribed and physically extant, such objects carry an evidential weight that no legend can match.

**F**
A second channel ran into what is now central Vietnam. The kingdom of Champa, culturally Indic rather than Chinese, received Buddhist influence directly from India across the Bay of Bengal. In 875 a large Buddhist monastery was founded at Đồng Dương, in the Cham capital of Indrapura, under royal patronage; its sculpture and iconography display features associated with the esoteric traditions then developing in eastern India. Champa thus supplies evidence of tantric influence on the territory of modern Vietnam roughly a century before the Hoa Lư pillars, though by a different route and within a different polity.

**G**
The subsequent history is one of repeated reorganisation. In 1293 the emperor Trần Nhân Tông gave up his throne, was ordained at Yên Tử in 1299, and founded the Trúc Lâm lineage, the first school with a distinctively Vietnamese character; from this period onward Buddhist, Confucian and Daoist elements were held together in a working synthesis. A revival movement in the 1920s and 1930s reorganised monastic education, established printing houses and created lay associations. In 1981 the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha was formed, bringing earlier organisations under a single structure. Vietnam has since hosted the United Nations Day of Vesak twice, most recently in Ho Chi Minh City in May 2025, attended by delegates from more than eighty countries, during which a relic from Sarnath in India was displayed at four sites for roughly twenty days. Quantifying any of this is harder than it looks: official statistics record only a few million people as Buddhists, because the question asked concerns exclusive affiliation, whereas a great deal of Vietnamese practice is not exclusive at all.

**H**
Why, then, does the inflated chronology survive? Two ordinary reasons, neither of them dishonest: a natural local pride in antiquity, and a habit of reading that treats legend and documentary record as though they were the same kind of evidence. The correction is worth making, because the accurate account is the richer one. It shows a tradition that has been imported repeatedly rather than once — by sea in the second century, through dhāraṇī texts in the sixth, through court ritual in the tenth, through Champa from the south, and through global networks today. A tradition that has been re-imported this often is a good deal more interesting than one that arrived and stood still.
