# READING PASSAGE

### Reconstructing the Life of Atiśa

**A**
Few figures in Tibetan religious history are described as confidently as Atiśa Dīpaṃkara Śrījñāna (982–1054), the scholar from Bengal whose arrival in western Tibet in 1042 is conventionally taken to mark the turning point of the "later diffusion" of Buddhism. Readers are told that he was born a prince, studied at the monastery of Vikramaśīla, spent twelve years in Sumatra, was summoned to Tibet by a king who died in captivity rather than ransom himself, and there restored a corrupted religion to its proper form. This article argues that most of that portrait is a later construction, that a smaller and more interesting figure survives beneath it, and that the two should not be confused.

**B**
The problem begins with the sources. No Indian record of Atiśa survives; everything known about him comes from Tibet, and most of it from biographies compiled by the Kadam school that his disciple Dromtön (1005–1064) founded after his death. The earliest of these accounts may draw on the recollections of people who knew him, but the fullest versions were written down generations later, when Atiśa had already become the founding saint of a monastic order. Such texts are not fabrications. They are, however, documents of devotion, and they follow the conventions of Indian and Tibetan hagiography: a royal birth, prophetic dreams, a search for teachers, miracles on the road. Where a detail serves those conventions, historians ask whether it also served the facts.

**C**
The disagreement is sharpest over the invitation. The traditional account holds that Yeshe Ö, the monk-king of Guge, was captured by a rival ruler and, offered his freedom for his weight in gold, instructed his nephew Jangchub Ö to spend the gold instead on bringing Atiśa from India. Critical historians have doubted the story on two grounds: it is absent from the earliest layers of the biographical tradition, and Yeshe Ö's death is recorded differently elsewhere. It is worth being precise about the claim. The sceptics do not argue that the invitation never happened, only that the ransom is a later addition. The invitation itself is well attested, and it fits what is known of Guge, whose rulers had been sending young men to Kashmir for training as translators since the late tenth century.

**D**
On the second contested point, whether Atiśa came to "reform" Tibetan Buddhism, the present writer inclines to a middle position. That tantric practice in Tibet was thought to have gone astray is not a Kadam invention: Yeshe Ö himself issued an ordinance around 985 condemning ritual sex and killing in the name of the tantras. But strictly speaking, whether Atiśa reformed Tibetan religion and whether Tibetans believed he did are different questions. His own writings are cautious rather than polemical, and the Kadam reputation for discipline owes at least as much to Dromtön, a layman who never took full ordination, as to the master himself. Reconstruction would be the better word than reform: Atiśa arranged existing teachings into a graded sequence that Tibetans found usable.

**E**
That sequence is the one product of his visit whose authorship nobody questions. At the request of Jangchub Ö, Atiśa composed at Tholing the Bodhipathapradīpa, the Lamp for the Path to Enlightenment, a verse text of 68 stanzas. Its organising device is a division of practitioners into three capacities. The person of lesser capacity seeks only a better rebirth; the person of middling capacity seeks personal liberation; the person of superior capacity seeks enlightenment for the sake of all beings, and it is for this third type that the remainder of the text, on the awakening mind, ethics, meditation and the correct place of tantra, is written. The scheme was later expanded by Tsongkhapa into the Gelug "stages of the path" literature, which is why a text of a few pages has had an influence out of all proportion to its length.

**F**
The journey that produced the Lamp can be reconstructed in some detail, and here the sources agree. Jangchub Ö dispatched the translator Nagtso Tsultrim Gyalwa to Vikramaśīla with a quantity of gold and a formal request. Nagtso waited, reportedly for many months, before the request could be put to the abbot, Ratnākara, who consented only on condition that Atiśa return to India within three years. The party travelled through Nepal, where Atiśa stayed for about a year, and reached Tholing in 1042. Atiśa never did return: the road south was reported unsafe, and in 1045 he met Dromtön and moved to central Tibet, where he died at Nyethang, near Lhasa, in 1054. Dromtön founded Reting monastery in 1056, and the Kadam school was born.

**G**
Even the bare dates carry a definitional problem. Tibetan sources give Atiśa's age at death as seventy-two, but Tibetan reckoning counts a person as one year old at birth and adds a year at each new year, so the same figure yields a birth date of 980 or 982 depending on the convention applied. The Sumatran episode raises a similar difficulty. That Atiśa studied under Dharmakīrti of Suvarṇadvīpa, known in Tibet as Serlingpa, and that he credited this teacher with his understanding of the awakening mind, is affirmed in works attributed to Atiśa himself; the figure of twelve years, by contrast, is a round number that recurs in Buddhist biography and may be conventional rather than counted.

**H**
None of this diminishes Atiśa. It is precisely because he was important that his life was retold, and the retelling is itself historical evidence of what the Kadam school wished its founder to be. The fair conclusion is that the Atiśa of the biographies and the Atiśa of the documents are two figures who overlap but are not identical: the first a saint with a complete life, the second a scholar with a verifiable itinerary, one indisputable text and a disciple who did more than anyone to make him remembered. Whether the ransom story will ever be settled is doubtful, since the sources that could settle it were written by people who had already decided.
