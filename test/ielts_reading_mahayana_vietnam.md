# Mahāyāna Buddhism in Vietnam

> **Về chủ đề**
> Đại thừa (Bắc tông) là dòng chiếm đa số áp đảo ở Việt Nam, nên viết theo kiểu liệt kê niên đại sẽ nhạt và trùng với Workbook #3. Bài này đi vào một **tranh luận học thuật có thật**: Phật giáo Việt Nam vẫn được gọi là truyền thống **Thiền**, nhưng cái diễn ra hằng ngày trong đa số chùa lại là **niệm Phật, cúng vong, lễ Vu Lan**. Khoảng cách giữa cái tên và cái thực hành ấy là chủ đề của bài.
>
> Trục tranh luận: cuốn **Thiền uyển tập anh** (1337) — nguồn sử liệu gốc dựng nên hình ảnh "Phật giáo Việt Nam là Thiền" — bị công trình của **Cuong Tu Nguyen** (*Zen in Medieval Vietnam*, University of Hawai'i Press, 1997) đặt lại vấn đề. Đây là loại lập luận sử học điển hình của Passage 3, và cũng là kiến thức đáng biết.

> **Cách dùng:** cùng hệ thống 1–86 và 16 dạng như bốn workbook trước.

# READING PASSAGE

### The Name and the Practice: Mahāyāna Buddhism in Vietnam

**A**
Vietnamese Buddhism is habitually described, at home and abroad, as a Thiền or Zen tradition — a lineage of meditation masters transmitting insight from one generation to the next. Walk into an average Mahāyāna temple in Vietnam, however, and meditation is not what you will find. You will hear the recitation of the name of Amitābha Buddha, watch rites performed on behalf of the dead, and find, behind the main hall, an ancestor hall holding the tablets of the deceased. The gap between the name a tradition is given and the activity that actually fills its buildings is not a minor untidiness. It is the most interesting problem in the study of Vietnamese Buddhism, and it has a long history of its own.

**B**
The genealogical account has a datable beginning. In 1096 the queen mother of the Lý court asked when Buddhism had entered the country and by what line it had been transmitted. The monk Thông Biện answered her, and his answer is the earliest surviving attempt to give Vietnamese Buddhism a pedigree. The fuller version appears in the *Thiền uyển tập anh*, compiled in 1337 and written, like all serious Vietnamese writing of the period, in Literary Chinese. It records three lineages: one traced to Vinītaruci, who reached Luy Lâu in 580; one to Vô Ngôn Thông, dated to 820; and one to Thảo Đường, dated to 1069. For six hundred years this was the framework within which Vietnamese Buddhists understood their own past.

**C**
The text then very nearly disappeared. It survived in a small number of manuscript copies, and it was only in 1927 that the scholar Trần Văn Giáp located one in a private library, translated it and introduced it to a modern readership. The timing mattered enormously. Vietnamese intellectuals were then engaged in constructing a national history, and here was a document that supplied Buddhism with an unbroken indigenous descent. A widely read history published in the 1970s treated its account as reliable, and from that point the lineage narrative became the standard view — repeated in monastic education, in popular writing and in the self-description of temples that in practice did nothing resembling meditation.

**D**
In 1997 a study published by the University of Hawai'i Press subjected the text to close scrutiny and reached uncomfortable conclusions. Its author, Cuong Tu Nguyen, argued that the *Thiền uyển tập anh* belongs to a recognisable Chinese literary genre — the transmission-of-the-lamp record — whose function was to establish legitimacy by demonstrating descent, not to preserve an account of what had happened. On this reading the three lineages cannot be historically verified, and a Zen tradition in the institutional sense never existed in Vietnam at all. It is worth being precise about the claim: he does not argue that the text was fabricated, only that it should be read as the genre it belongs to. Critics reply that a document which fails as chronicle may still succeed as evidence of something else, namely what the Buddhist elite of the fourteenth century wished to be able to claim. Both sides, in the end, are arguing about what kind of evidence a genealogy is.

**E**
What, meanwhile, was actually going on in the temples? A combination, and by all indications it always had been. Meditation, Pure Land recitation and esoteric elements were practised together rather than as competing options; Vietnamese monks have long used the phrase *thiền tịnh song tu*, the joint cultivation of meditation and the Pure Land. The bulk of a temple's activity, then as now, consisted of ritual services for the dead, culminating in the great festival of the seventh lunar month, at which merit is transferred to deceased relatives. To describe this as a decline from some purer earlier condition is to assume the very thing in question. There is no period at which Vietnamese Mahāyāna was a meditation tradition and nothing else.

**F**
The clearest illustration is Trúc Lâm, the one school with an unambiguously Vietnamese identity. Its founder was the emperor Trần Nhân Tông, who abdicated and was ordained at Yên Tử in 1299; he is counted with Pháp Loa and Huyền Quang as the school's three patriarchs. Whatever its medieval standing, its transmission did not continue unbroken. The Trúc Lâm one encounters in Vietnam today dates from the 1970s, when the monk Thích Thanh Từ set out deliberately to revive it, and it acquired its most visible institution when a large meditation centre opened at Đà Lạt in 1994. This is regularly described as a restoration. Reconstruction would be the better word — and the enterprise is recognisably the same one the medieval genealogists were engaged in, which is to say the assembly of a usable lineage.

**G**
A final version of the tradition was assembled for export. Thích Nhất Hạnh, ordained at Từ Hiếu in Huế, left Vietnam in 1966 and did not return for thirty-nine years; in 1982 he founded the practice centre known as Plum Village in France, and he eventually died at the temple where he had begun. What he offered international audiences was mindfulness practice together with a socially committed reading of the precepts, and it travelled extremely well. What did not travel was the surrounding matrix — the recitation, the ancestor hall, the rites for the dead — so that readers abroad received a version of Vietnamese Buddhism from which most of Vietnamese Buddhism had been removed. There is an additional irony here. Writing earlier under the name Nguyễn Lang, the same author had produced the influential history that helped fix the lineage account as orthodoxy.

**H**
The recurrence is the point. In the eleventh century a court wanted a pedigree; in the twentieth, nationalist intellectuals wanted a Buddhism that was demonstrably Vietnamese rather than borrowed from China; in the late twentieth, foreign readers wanted meditation unencumbered by ritual. Each audience needed a single clean strand, and each was duly supplied with one. The practice, throughout, has remained stubbornly plural. Vietnamese Mahāyāna has not been misdescribed by accident or through carelessness; it has been misdescribed because a description that fitted it would have been of no use to anybody who wanted one.
