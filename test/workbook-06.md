# READING PASSAGE

### Counting the Schools of Tibet

**A**
Almost any introductory guide will say that Tibetan Buddhism has four schools: the Nyingma, the Kagyu, the Sakya and the Gelug. The formula is repeated on museum labels, in Dharma centres abroad and in the administrative categories of the Tibetan exile government. It is not wrong, exactly. But the number four, this article argues, tells us at least as much about how Tibetan religion has been described as about how it was practised, and whether it is the right number divides historians in instructive ways.

**B**
The historical scaffolding is not itself in dispute. Buddhism was adopted under the Tibetan empire, and the monastery of Samye, traditionally dated to 779, was founded under the patronage of King Trisong Detsen and associated with the tantric master Padmasambhava. The empire fragmented after the assassination of the king Langdarma around 842, and for more than a century monastic institutions largely collapsed. What Tibetans call the later diffusion began in the eleventh century: the Indian scholar Atiśa arrived in 1042, the translator Marpa (1012–1097) made repeated journeys to India, and the Sakya monastery was founded in 1073. From this period comes the one distinction that nobody contests: between the Nyingma, the "old translation" school claiming the imperial-era texts, and the Sarma or "new translation" schools built on the later Indian imports.

**C**
The first view, associated with traditional Tibetan doxography, holds that the four schools reflect real and durable differences of transmission. The Nyingma preserve the Dzogchen teachings and a distinctive body of terma, texts said to have been concealed by Padmasambhava and revealed centuries later. The Kagyu trace their Mahāmudrā instructions from the Indian siddha Tilopa through Nāropa to Marpa, then to the poet-yogi Milarepa and to Gampopa, who joined those instructions to the Kadam monastic curriculum and from whom the Karma Kagyu and other branches descend. The Sakya are organised around the Lamdré, the "path and its fruit" system, and the Gelug around the scholastic synthesis of Tsongkhapa (1357–1419), who founded Ganden monastery in 1409. The Gelug are often called the "reformed" school; consolidation would be the better word, since Tsongkhapa rejected no tantric material, only its practice without prior monastic training.

**D**
The second view, more common among academic historians, holds that the fourfold list is a late convention that flattens a more crowded landscape. The Kadam school of Atiśa's disciples was absorbed into the Gelug. The Jonang, whose "other-emptiness" philosophy was condemned as heterodox, had their central Tibetan monasteries converted in the seventeenth century, after the Fifth Dalai Lama gained political authority in 1642, and survive only in the eastern region of Amdo. The Bodong and Shangpa Kagyu traditions rarely appear in the lists. Nor is four a stable number even among Tibetans: the exile administration has since the late 1970s counted five traditions, adding the pre-Buddhist Bön, and the nineteenth-century Rimé or "non-sectarian" movement produced a different tally altogether. Jamgön Kongtrül (1813–1899), its leading compiler, organised his Treasury of Knowledge around eight practice lineages rather than four schools.

**E**
It is worth being precise about the claim. The second view does not argue that the schools are inventions, only that the number is. The Nyingma and the Gelug are as real as any institution in Tibet; what is conventional is the decision to draw the map with exactly four regions and to treat everything else as a footnote. Strictly speaking, "school" and "lineage" are different questions. A school is an institution, with monasteries, an abbot and a curriculum; a lineage is a chain of transmission from teacher to student, and a single lineage may run through several schools. The present writer inclines to the second view, though with a concession: the Nyingma–Sarma divide is old, doctrinally substantive and recognised by the traditions themselves, and no revision of the count should obscure it.

**F**
One reason to doubt that the schools were ever sealed compartments is that their most characteristic institutions were shared. The recognition of reincarnate lamas, or tulkus, is usually said to begin with Karma Pakshi (1204–1283), acknowledged as the rebirth of Düsum Khyenpa, founder of Tsurphu monastery in 1189, and it later spread to every school. The procedure became uniform. When a senior lama dies, his attendants look for a prediction letter or for signs he left. A search party then consults oracles and sometimes the visions seen in a sacred lake. Candidate children are tested with the predecessor's possessions. A senior hierarch confirms the recognition, the child is enthroned at the predecessor's seat, and a long education follows. The title Dalai Lama itself, conferred by the Mongol ruler Altan Khan in 1578, was attached to a lineage of this kind.

**G**
The canon was equally common property, and it illustrates a general problem of counting. The Kangyur, the collected words of the Buddha, and the Tengyur, the Indian commentaries, were catalogued by the Sakya scholar Butön (1290–1364) and used by all schools. Yet the Derge edition of the Kangyur, printed in 1733, runs to 103 volumes, while other editions differ, because editors disagreed about what to include and how to divide it. The number of volumes depends on the grain of the division, not only on the texts. The number of schools behaves the same way: the Kagyu are conventionally said to have four major and eight minor branches, and the Nyingma six "mother monasteries", and whether these are units or sub-units is a decision, not a discovery.

**H**
None of this makes the fourfold scheme useless. It is teachable, it is the framework Tibetans now most often use, and any replacement would face the same problem of where to draw lines. The fair conclusion is that "four" should be read as a map and not as the territory: a serviceable simplification of a plural tradition, adopted for good reasons and then mistaken for a fact of nature. Whether historians and doxographers will ever agree is doubtful, since their disagreement is about categories rather than events, and categories are not settled by finding new documents.
