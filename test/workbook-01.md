# READING PASSAGE

### The Long Road: How Buddhism Crossed Asia

**A**
When Siddhartha Gautama began teaching in the Ganges basin around the fifth century BCE, nothing about his movement suggested that it would one day reach the Pacific. His earliest followers were wandering renunciants who owned little more than a robe and a begging bowl, and who were instructed to depend on lay communities for their daily food. A tradition founded on homelessness, poverty and detachment from worldly affairs seems an improbable candidate for continental expansion. Yet within a thousand years Buddhist monasteries stood in Afghanistan, Java, Korea and Japan, and Buddhist texts had been rendered into more languages than any other body of literature in the ancient world.

**B**
The conventional explanation begins with Ashoka, the Mauryan emperor who ruled much of the Indian subcontinent in the third century BCE. Later Buddhist chronicles, most of them composed in Sri Lanka several centuries after his death, present him as a repentant conqueror who was so appalled by the slaughter of his own campaign in Kalinga that he embraced the dharma and despatched missionaries as far as Sri Lanka and the Greek-ruled kingdoms to the west. Ashoka's own inscriptions, carved into rocks and pillars across his territory, complicate this portrait considerably. They speak warmly of moral conduct, of generosity towards ascetics of every persuasion, and of an official policy of non-violence — but they rarely refer to the specific doctrines that distinguish Buddhism from its rivals. A number of historians consequently argue that the chronicles project a later sectarian enthusiasm back onto a ruler whose religious interests were in fact considerably broader.

**C**
Whatever Ashoka's private convictions may have been, the trade routes his empire helped to secure mattered enormously. Buddhism did not spread the way an army spreads; it spread the way goods spread. Monasteries were established at oasis towns along the arid corridors linking northern India to Central Asia, where they functioned simultaneously as places of worship, banks, warehouses and rest houses for caravans. The relationship was reciprocal: merchants required safe lodging and a moral community that would honour contracts far from home, while monks required donors. Donative inscriptions surviving at Buddhist sites in western India confirm the point — traders and their wives appear among the most frequent contributors, far outnumbering kings.

**D**
Movement across geography, however, entailed movement across languages, and here the difficulties were formidable. When Buddhist ideas first reached China in the early centuries CE, translators confronted a philosophical vocabulary for which Chinese had no equivalents. Their initial solution, known as *geyi* or 'matching concepts', was to borrow terminology from Daoism: *nirvana* was rendered using the Daoist term *wuwei*, or 'non-action'. The results were frequently misleading, and later generations condemned them. Kumārajīva, a Central Asian monk brought to the Chinese capital of Chang'an in 401 CE after nearly two decades of captivity, produced translations of such elegance that they displaced almost everything written before him and remain in liturgical use today. Yet a growing number of scholars now suggest that the supposed errors of the earlier period were productive rather than merely defective: by clothing unfamiliar ideas in familiar dress, they made those ideas thinkable at all.

**E**
Traffic along these routes ran in both directions. In the late 620s the Chinese monk Xuanzang, frustrated by the inconsistencies he found among the translations available to him, left China without official permission and travelled overland to India, where he remained for over a decade before returning in 645 with hundreds of manuscripts. His account of the journey is today consulted less by students of Chinese religion than by historians of India, for whom it supplies dates, place names and descriptions of institutions that indigenous Indian sources, which show comparatively little interest in chronology, simply do not record.

**F**
The religion also transformed the technologies it encountered. Buddhist doctrine holds that reproducing a sacred text generates merit for the person who commissions it, and this created a demand for copies on a scale no purely literary culture had ever required. The earliest surviving printed book bearing a date is a Chinese edition of the Diamond Sutra, produced in 868 CE and recovered in 1907 from a sealed chamber in the cave complex at Dunhuang. Its colophon records that it was commissioned by a man named Wang Jie for free distribution, on behalf of his parents. Printing, in other words, was refined in the service of devotion long before it was applied to commerce or administration.

**G**
The final irony is that Buddhism eventually all but disappeared from the land of its birth. By the thirteenth century the great monastic universities of the Ganges plain lay in ruins and the tradition had ceased to be a significant presence in Indian religious life, even as it flourished from Tibet to Japan. Explanations remain contested. Some emphasise the withdrawal of royal patronage; others point to the absorption of Buddhist devotional practice into an expanding Hindu landscape; others still to the military campaigns that destroyed Nalanda and comparable institutions in the 1190s. Because monasteries depended on external donation in a way that household-based traditions did not, they may simply have been more fragile once their patrons vanished.

**H**
What unites much recent scholarship is a shift of emphasis. An older generation of historians tended to explain the spread of religions by the appeal of their ideas. The current tendency is to look first at the material conditions that carried those ideas: the roads, the donors, the storerooms, the scribes. Buddhism travelled, on this account, not chiefly because it persuaded, but because it was carried — though the two explanations are, of course, harder to separate than either camp usually admits.
