# READING PASSAGE

### Remaking a Tradition: Tibetan Buddhism in the Twenty-First Century

**A**
Few religious traditions have become globally visible as rapidly as Tibetan Buddhism. Its texts are translated into dozens of languages, its meditation techniques are studied in university laboratories, and its most senior figure has been among the most widely recognised religious personalities of the past half-century. It would be easy to read this prominence as evidence of institutional health. The reality is more complicated. The same decades that carried the tradition into Western universities also severed it from the monastic economy, the patronage networks and the geographical setting that had sustained it for centuries. Visibility and security, in this case, are not the same measurement.

**B**
The rupture came in 1959, when the fourteenth Dalai Lama and tens of thousands of followers crossed into India; roughly 130,000 Tibetans now live outside Tibet, with the exile administration based in the northern Indian town of Dharamshala. The three great Gelug monastic universities — Sera, Drepung and Ganden — were re-established from the 1960s onwards in southern India, on land made available by the Indian government. This achievement is often described as preservation, but the word understates what was involved. Libraries had been lost, and portions of the syllabus had to be reconstructed from the memory of senior scholars. Nor were the new institutions simply the old ones relocated: they served a scattered population, depended on international donation rather than local estates, and taught in a country whose legal and educational environment was entirely unlike the one they had left.

**C**
The textual situation was equally precarious, since a great deal of Tibetan literature had survived in only a handful of copies. The response has been an ambitious programme of digitisation. The organisation now known as the Buddhist Digital Resource Center was founded in 1999 by the American scholar E. Gene Smith, under the earlier name of the Tibetan Buddhist Resource Center, and has since made millions of pages freely available. The consequences reach further than convenience. A manuscript that once existed in a single monastery, access to which was regulated by that monastery, can now be consulted by anyone with a network connection. Some scholars welcome this redistribution of access unreservedly. Others observe that Tibetan texts were traditionally read alongside an oral commentary transmitted from teacher to student, and worry that the written word is now circulating detached from the interpretative apparatus that once accompanied it.

**D**
A second transformation has occurred inside the curriculum itself. Following an invitation from the Dalai Lama, a science programme was developed and piloted over several years in collaboration with an American university. In 2013 senior monastic leaders in the Gelug tradition approved it, and from the following year a six-year curriculum in physics, biology and neuroscience, supplemented by mathematics and the philosophy of science, was introduced at the three largest monastic universities in southern India. Commentators have called this the most substantial alteration to the monastic syllabus in six hundred years. Selected monastics have since studied abroad and returned as teachers, the intention being that the programme should eventually become sustainable without external staffing. Critics have asked whether importing a foreign epistemology risks diluting the tradition; the more persuasive reply is that formal debate has been the backbone of monastic training for centuries, so that a curriculum built on evidence and argument extends an existing habit of mind rather than contradicting it.

**E**
Change has also touched the position of women. In 2016, twenty nuns were awarded the Geshema, the highest scholastic degree in the Gelug system, the first women ever to hold it. The symbolic weight of this was considerable, and it has already altered what is expected of nunnery education. Yet it would be premature to describe the hierarchy as equalised. Full ordination on the same terms as monks remains unavailable within the Tibetan tradition, and nuns continue to be excluded from a number of ritual and administrative roles.

**F**
Meanwhile the tradition has acquired a large constituency abroad, organised around teaching centres whose members are mostly converts. This raises a problem of authority that the older system rarely had to solve. Within Tibet, a teacher's standing was underwritten by decades of visible institutional formation: everyone knew which college he had studied at and who had examined him. In a European or North American centre, that scaffolding is usually absent, and legitimacy tends to rest instead on personal charisma and reputation, which are far harder for students to verify. A series of well-documented failures of governance at Western centres over the past three decades has made the difficulty concrete rather than theoretical.

**G**
Overshadowing all of this is the question of succession. In 2011 the Dalai Lama transferred political authority to an elected leadership and registered the Gaden Phodrang Trust in Dharamshala; on 2 July 2025 he confirmed that the institution of the Dalai Lama would continue and stated that the Trust holds sole authority over the identification, education and well-being of his successor. The Chinese government does not accept this position, maintaining that recognition must follow domestic regulations issued in 2007, which require the historical golden urn procedure and central government approval. Whatever the outcome, a practical difficulty is unavoidable: a child recognised as a successor would need roughly two decades to reach maturity, leaving an interregnum during which the exile community's elected institutions, whatever their democratic merits, would have to function without the personal authority that has held the community together since 1959.

**H**
Scholarship on all this has shifted noticeably. An older literature treated the exile community as a museum, its value lying in the faithful conservation of something that had been destroyed elsewhere. Recent work describes instead a tradition undergoing active remaking — negotiating with digital media, with laboratory science, with new expectations about gender, and with a constituency that did not exist a century ago. The distinction matters, because the two framings imply different criteria for success. Judged as a museum, every departure from precedent is a loss; judged as a living system, adaptation is precisely the evidence that it is still alive.
