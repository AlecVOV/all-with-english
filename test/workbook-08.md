# Tsongkhapa and the Invention of the Gelug

> **Ghi chú cho người học**
> Tsongkhapa (1357–1419) thường được giới thiệu là "người sáng lập phái Gelug" và "nhà cải cách" Phật giáo Tây Tạng. Cả hai nhãn này đều có vấn đề: ngài không dùng chữ Gelug, không tuyên bố lập một tông phái, và tên gọi cùng bản sắc Gelug chỉ xuất hiện trong các thế hệ sau khi ngài mất. # READING PASSAGE

### Tsongkhapa and the Invention of the Gelug

**A**
Tsongkhapa Lobzang Drakpa (1357–1419) is routinely introduced as the founder of the Gelug school, the largest of the Tibetan Buddhist orders and the one to which the Dalai Lamas belong. The introduction is accurate in its effect and misleading in its wording. Tsongkhapa did not use the word Gelug, did not set out to establish an order, and would not have recognised in the sixteenth-century institution that bore the name a simple continuation of his own project. This article argues that the Gelug was assembled after his death by his disciples, that Tsongkhapa's own achievement was of a different and more scholarly kind, and that keeping the two apart clarifies rather than diminishes him.

**B**
The outline of the life is not disputed. He was born in 1357 at Tsongkha in Amdo, in the far north-east of the Tibetan world, and took his name from the place. Ordained as a novice in childhood, he travelled to central Tibet at about sixteen and spent the next two decades moving between monasteries of the Sakya, Kagyu and Kadam traditions, studying logic, monastic law, the philosophical treatises of India and the tantras. The most important of his many teachers was Rendawa Zhönu Lodrö (1349–1412), a Sakya scholar who taught him Madhyamaka philosophy and whom Tsongkhapa credited throughout his life. Nothing in this itinerary suggests a man preparing to break with the schools that trained him.

**C**
The traditional account, still found in Gelug histories, presents Tsongkhapa as a reformer who restored monastic discipline and corrected a tantric practice that had grown lax. A second view, common among modern historians, holds that this picture was drawn by his successors, who needed a founder to explain an order that already existed. It is worth being precise about the claim. The second view does not deny that Tsongkhapa founded Ganden monastery in 1409, only that he founded a school. The distinction rests on chronology: the name Gelug, "the virtuous ones", and the identity that went with it appear in the sources only in the generations after his death, when his followers were first called Gandenpa, "those of Ganden", and later needed a label that covered Drepung and Sera as well.

**D**
The present writer inclines to the second view, but with a concession. The year 1409 was a founding moment in any sense of the word. In the first month of that year Tsongkhapa instituted the Great Prayer Festival at Lhasa, gathering thousands of monks from all traditions around the Jokhang temple, and later that year he established Ganden on Wangbur hill, about forty kilometres east of the city. Two further monasteries followed within his lifetime, both founded by his students: Drepung in 1416 and Sera in 1419, the second by Jamchen Chöje Shakya Yeshe. What Tsongkhapa did not do was give these houses a name, a curriculum of their own or a rule that set them apart. Innovator would be the better word than reformer: the novelty lay in what he taught, not in the institutions he left.

**E**
That teaching is preserved in a body of writing usually reckoned at eighteen volumes, and two works dominate it. The Lamrim Chenmo, the Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path, was completed at Reting in 1402; it takes the threefold scheme of Atiśa's Lamp and expands it into a manual several hundred pages long. Its companion, the Ngagrim Chenmo, does the same for tantra. What made Tsongkhapa controversial in his own day was neither work but his reading of Madhyamaka, the "middle way" philosophy of Nāgārjuna, in which he insisted that emptiness and dependent origination were not rival accounts of reality but the same account seen twice. Sakya and Kagyu scholars attacked this position for centuries, which is the best evidence that it was genuinely new.

**F**
Ganden itself acquired its later shape only after its founder's death. When Tsongkhapa died there in 1419, his body was placed in a reliquary in the main hall. His disciple Gyaltsab Je (1364–1432) then took the throne of Ganden as his successor. On Gyaltsab's retirement the seat passed to Khedrub Je (1385–1438), who also composed the biography from which most later accounts descend. After Khedrub the office, known as the Ganden Tripa, was filled by senior scholars in turn rather than by a reincarnated lama, a rule that has held ever since. The monastery was divided into two colleges, Jangtse and Shartse, each with its own teaching lineage. Every element of this structure except the reliquary postdates the man it honours.

**G**
Strictly speaking, whether Tsongkhapa was a reformer and whether reform was needed are different questions, and the sources answer the second more clearly than the first. Tsongkhapa's insistence that tantric initiation be preceded by full monastic training was real, and it distinguished his followers from many contemporaries. But the invitation he received from the Yongle Emperor of China in 1408, which he declined, sending Shakya Yeshe to the Ming court in his place, shows a scholar already famous across Asia for learning rather than for discipline. Reform is what an order remembers about its founder; learning is what his opponents remembered about him.

**H**
None of this makes the founder's title wrong so much as retrospective. Institutions need founders, and the disciples who built the Gelug were not dishonest in choosing Tsongkhapa: no one else could have served. The fair conclusion is that the founder of the Gelug is a figure created by the Gelug, standing in front of a philosopher who wrote in 1402 and 1409 without knowing what would be built on the result. Whether Gelug historians and their academic critics will ever agree is doubtful, since one side is describing an origin and the other a process, and the same events can be both.
