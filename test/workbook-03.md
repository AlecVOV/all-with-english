# Theravāda Buddhism in Vietnam

> **Về chữ "Tiểu thừa" — đọc trước khi làm**
> "Tiểu thừa" dịch từ **Hīnayāna**, một nhãn mang tính **luận chiến** do phía Đại thừa đặt ra ("cỗ xe nhỏ"). Nó không phải tên tự xưng của bất kỳ tông phái nào. Năm **1950**, hội nghị sáng lập **World Fellowship of Buddhists** tại **Colombo** đã thống nhất không dùng từ này để chỉ Theravāda nữa.
> Cách gọi đúng trong tiếng Việt: **Nam tông**, **Phật giáo Nguyên thủy**, hoặc **Theravāda**. Bài đọc dưới đây dùng "Theravāda", và lấy luôn vấn đề thuật ngữ này làm điểm mở bài.
>
> Một điểm quan trọng hơn về mặt nội dung: Theravāda ở Việt Nam **không phải một khối**, mà là **hai cộng đồng gần như không liên quan nhau về mặt lịch sử** — Nam tông Khmer (rất cổ) và Nam tông Kinh (chỉ có từ thế kỷ XX). Gộp chung lại dưới một cái tên là bỏ mất phần thú vị nhất.

# READING PASSAGE

### Two Arrivals: Theravāda Buddhism in Vietnam

**A**
Vietnamese usage still commonly refers to Theravāda Buddhism as *Tiểu thừa*, a translation of the Sanskrit *Hīnayāna*, or 'lesser vehicle'. The label is polemical: it was coined by proponents of the Mahāyāna and has never been used by the tradition it describes. At its founding conference in Colombo in 1950, the World Fellowship of Buddhists agreed that the term should be abandoned. The objection is not merely one of courtesy. The word implies a single subordinate thing, whereas what exists on Vietnamese territory is two Theravāda communities whose histories have almost nothing in common: one that has been present for some fifteen centuries, and one that arrived within living memory.

**B**
The older community is Khmer. The Mekong Delta was settled by Khmer speakers long before it was Vietnamese; the Vietnamese advance southward brought the region under Vietnamese administration only from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Theravāda was therefore established on this land before the land became Vietnam, a sequence that is easy to state and easy to forget. The Khmer Krom population today numbers roughly 1.3 million, concentrated in provinces such as Trà Vinh, Sóc Trăng and An Giang, and maintains more than 450 pagodas served by something in the order of 8,500 monks.

**C**
What distinguishes these institutions is the range of what they do. A Khmer pagoda is a place of worship, but it is also the principal venue for community festivals, the site at which young men undertake ordination that is usually temporary rather than lifelong, and — most consequentially — the place where the Khmer script and the Pali language are taught. Since formal schooling in the delta is conducted in Vietnamese, the pagoda has become the main institution through which a written script is transmitted from one generation to the next. This gives it a role in maintaining identity that Mahāyāna temples serving the Kinh majority simply do not have. A Khmer Theravāda Buddhist Academy was opened at Cần Thơ in 2006.

**D**
The second arrival could hardly be more different. Theravāda reached the Vietnamese-speaking majority only in the twentieth century, and by a strikingly indirect route. Lê Văn Giảng, educated in Hanoi and employed as a veterinary officer, was posted by the French colonial administration to Phnom Penh. There he encountered Cambodian monks, learned meditation on the breath, and eventually ordained under the name Hộ Tông. Around 1940, at the invitation of a group of lay supporters, he returned and helped establish Bửu Quang temple at Gò Dưa, on the edge of Saigon: the first temple in the country where Theravāda was taught in Vietnamese. The tradition thus reached the Kinh majority neither from Sri Lanka nor from Burma, but sideways, through Cambodia and through the machinery of colonial employment.

**E**
Institutional consolidation followed quickly. Kỳ Viên temple, built in Saigon in 1949–50, became the movement's centre, and in 1957 the Vietnamese Theravāda Buddhist Sangha Congregation was formally established with Hộ Tông as its first president. Temples were founded in Huế, Đà Nẵng and elsewhere. Then came an episode that reads almost as a settling of accounts. The Cambodian monastic order had been destroyed under the Khmer Rouge, and in 1979 a group of Vietnamese bhikkhus travelled to Phnom Penh to re-ordain Cambodian monks, thereby helping to re-establish the very lineage from which their own ordination had originally derived. A tradition borrowed from Cambodia in the 1930s was returned to it forty years later.

**F**
A quieter development has arguably mattered more. Thích Minh Châu, a Vietnamese scholar-monk who took his doctorate in India, translated the Pali collections into Vietnamese over several decades. The effect was textual rather than institutional, and it extended well beyond the Theravāda community: for the first time, Vietnamese readers whose own tradition was Mahāyāna could consult the early canonical material directly in their own language rather than through Chinese intermediaries. Meditation methods associated with Theravāda have since become widespread in temples that would not describe themselves as Theravāda at all. The tradition's influence, in other words, is poorly indexed by the number of monks who formally belong to it.

**G**
Vietnam also produced a school that fits neither category. The Khất sĩ or 'mendicant' movement, founded in 1944 by Minh Đăng Quang, adopted the daily alms round and much of the outward monastic form associated with Theravāda, while retaining Mahāyāna doctrine and conducting everything in Vietnamese. It became one of the nine organisations brought together when the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha was formed in 1981, and it remains substantial. Its existence is a standing difficulty for anyone who wants to sort Vietnamese Buddhism into two tidy boxes, and a useful reminder that the categories were produced by scholars and polemicists rather than by practitioners.

**H**
How, then, should the place of Theravāda in Vietnam be assessed? Counting institutions gives one answer and understates the case badly. A better measure would take account of the canonical texts now available in Vietnamese and read across sectarian lines, of meditation practices that have spread far beyond their point of entry, and of a monastic form that a wholly indigenous movement chose to adopt. On that measure the tradition is not a minority appendage to Vietnamese Buddhism but one of the currents that has shaped it. The old term, whatever else is wrong with it, gets this precisely backwards.
